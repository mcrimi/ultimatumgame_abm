How much can you take?

Sixty players take turns claiming a share of each other’s money. Claim too much and your victim burns the lot. Greed pushes claims up, shame pulls them down, and a society finds its price.

The rules

  1. Each turn the players are shuffled into pairs: one proposer, one responder.
  2. The proposer claims a share of the responder’s money: their take rate.
  3. If the claim is within what the responder tolerates, it is paid, and greed nudges the proposer’s take rate up for next time.
  4. If it is too much, the responder destroys all their own money so the proposer gets nothing, and shame cuts the proposer’s take rate.
  5. Everyone earns interest. A player with no money cannot be taken from, and one with too little cannot propose.

Economists call this a power-to-take game, a harsher cousin of the ultimatum game. It was used by Reuben and van Winden to study what proposers feel after being punished: those who thought they had taken unfairly felt shame and guilt, and claimed less the next time. This model takes that one finding and asks what it does to a whole society, turn after turn.

What happens

1. The first turn is a massacre

With the original settings, proposers start out claiming 80% and responders tolerate about 50%. Every responder says no. In one turn 30 of the 60 players burn everything they own.

The lesson costs the proposers nothing. They keep their money, feel their shame, and come back claiming 32%. It is the people they tried to rob who pay for their education.

2. Then claims saw their way up to the limit, for ever

A chastened proposer does not stay humble. Each accepted claim makes the next one a little bigger, until it crosses a responder’s limit, is rejected, and collapses again. The take rate never settles: it climbs slowly and falls off a cliff, over and over. Network engineers will recognise the shape. It is how TCP finds the capacity of a link.

Because the ups and downs have to cancel in the long run, the share of claims that end in rejection is fixed by shame and greed alone:

rejection rate = log(1 + greed) / ( log(1 + greed) − log(1 − shame) )

How tolerant the responders are does not appear in it. Tolerance decides how high the saw climbs, not how often it falls. With the original settings the formula gives 5.1%; the simulation gives 5.0%.

Shame has a second effect. A proposer who drops a long way after each rejection spends most of the time well below what they could get away with. With the original settings responders would tolerate 50%, but the average claim is only about 33%. Shame protects responders twice: fewer of them are ruined, and those who are not are taken for less.

3. Without a safety net, the game dies

Interest on nothing is nothing, so a ruined player never recovers. Every rejection removes someone for good, and after a hundred turns about 48 of the 60 are out. The few left standing own everything.

Give every player a small sum each turn and the picture changes. Nobody is out for long, all thirty pairs keep playing, and the wealth is spread far more evenly. The price is that ruined players come back with their original, shameless take rate and get someone else ruined before they learn.

How it was built

I first wrote this model in NetLogo in 2020. This version is a rewrite in Python: a NumPy engine that runs hundreds of worlds at once, the formula above worked out by hand and then checked against simulation, and a test suite.

The simulation at the top of this page is not a recording. It is the same model, ported to JavaScript and running in your browser. Both versions draw their randomness from one small generator built only from integer arithmetic, so given the same seed they produce the same world. A test replays trajectories exported from Python and requires the browser version to match them exactly, digit for digit.

model.py ── NumPy engine, many worlds at once
 ├─ theory.py        the rejection-rate formula
 ├─ experiments.py   sweeps over shame, greed, safety net
 └─ export.py        experiments.json ──→ charts on this page
                     fixtures.json    ──→ parity test
engine.js ── the same model in the browser (Python = JS, exactly)
